Final Project, Option 1

5 June 2026

David Lynch is often treated as someone who hides meaning, as if the strange rooms, damaged faces, delayed voices and sudden portals in his films were built to protect a secret that a clever enough viewer could eventually steal. This is especially tempting with Inland Empire, a film so full of doubled women, cursed scripts, rabbits, Polish hotel rooms, Hollywood sidewalks and digital shadows that it almost begs to be turned into a diagram. But Lynch’s late work seems much less interested in hiding one final meaning than in making meaning pass through forms that cannot hold it cleanly. In Catching the Big Fish, Lynch describes ideas as things caught by going deeper into consciousness, where fragments are not random because nothing is finally separate; “there couldn’t be a fragment that doesn’t relate to everything.” This sounds mystical, and it is, but it is also weirdly material. The spiritual is not floating above the work, it happens through the low-resolution digital surface of Inland Empire, through Laura Dern’s face becoming almost too open to remain a face, through a woman’s scream that does not fully turn into sound, through lithographic bodies that are lonely, written on, stuck in rooms or partially transformed into messages. Critics love saying that Lynch refuses explanation, which is true, but sometimes this makes his obscurity sound empty, as if not explaining were the same as not caring. In this essay, I want to read Lynch’s late esotericism as something more specific and more vulnerable: a language for universal love that appears only when the ordinary self starts to break apart. Reading Inland Empire alongside Catching the Big Fish and selected late lithographs, including Oh I Love You So Much, A Lonely Figure Talks to Himself Softly, I Write on Your Skin How Much I Love You and Man Floating in Room Alone, I argue that Lynch presents love not as psychological intimacy or romantic closure, but as a force that moves through damaged bodies, unstable images and not fully legible signs. Love in this late work, thus, feels closer to transmission, wound, prayer or release, and becomes visible when the self stops being whole enough to try and pretend it is alone.

Catching the Big Fish becomes useful here at the moment when it stops being treated too obediently. Lynch’s spiritual language can sound almost suspiciously simple, like someone trying to explain cinema by turning it into a self-help scheme of depth, happiness or creativity. And yet the book becomes stranger when read less as an answer to his films and more as a description of how an image travels before it becomes visible. For Lynch, ideas are caught by going deeper, into a level of consciousness where separateness starts to look temporary. This is the logic behind his famous fish metaphor, but the more important part is what it does to fragments. In the section on Inland Empire, he says directly, “There couldn’t be a fragment that doesn’t relate to everything.” The line is almost too perfect for a film that seems, at least on its surface, to refuse relation: Nikki and Sue, Hollywood and Poland, rabbits and hotel rooms, a cursed script next to a woman crying in front of a television. For Lynch, the fragment already belongs to something larger than the viewer can immediately organize.

We do not have to believe him when he talks about unity. His language still gives a model for how the work asks to be watched. Lynch says he made Inland Empire without a completed structure: “I didn’t have a script. I wrote the thing scene by scene, without much of a clue where it would end.” In a normal production context, this could sound irresponsible (or just romanticized auteur chaos). But he immediately gives this chaos this strange/charming spiritual mechanics: “I had this feeling that because all things are unified, this idea over here would somehow relate to that idea over there.” His esotericism becomes increasingly material here, because unity acts less like a theme and more like a visual pressure between images. When two things seem too far apart, Lynch says, their relation “conjures up a third thing that almost unifies those first two.” Meaning, then, happens between images, in the unstable space where the viewer feels a relation before being able to explain it.

The meditation language carries the same pressure. The Unified Field, for Lynch, is “pure bliss consciousness, absolute intelligence, and infinite creativity,” which could easily sound too clean for a film like Inland Empire. But the phrase he uses for meditation happiness is “not a goofball happiness, but a thick beauty.” Thick beauty is maybe what keeps the spiritual side from becoming embarrassing. It has weight, texture, almost a surface. This matters for our argument because Lynch also says that “anger and depression and sorrow are beautiful things in a story, but they’re like poison to the filmmaker or artist.” Darkness can be material, but it is not the final state of the person making the work. The negative state is something that can dissolve; “the clown suit starts to dissolve… Then, when it dissolves, you have freedom.” Read beside Inland Empire, this does not actually turn the film into a clean allegory of healing – the film is too dirty, too damaged and too full of women in trouble for that. Still, it suggests why Lynch’s late work keeps moving through nightmare as if nightmare were a passage instead of a home.

Cinema becomes the place where this can happen without turning into a statement. “Cinema is a language. It can say things – big, abstract things,” Lynch writes, and that language creates “a world, an experience, that people cannot have unless they see that film.” This is also why he insists that “a film should stand on its own” and that saying too much can “break the experience.” Viewers are still supposed to think, but thinking has to begin inside the film’s own kind of intelligence, closer to music, intuition and transmission than to any kind of verbal solution. Catching the Big Fish, then, does not decode Inland Empire, instead, it shows why the Lynchian obscurity keeps working: fragments floating on an ocean, damaged images still connected by something underneath, love or freedom appearing only as a force that moves through them.

Inland Empire makes Lynch’s faith in fragments uglier and more physical almost immediately. If Catching the Big Fish lets him speak of unity in strangely calm language, the film turns that calmness into a dirty surface, a broken entrance, a room that does not know whether it is a room or a trap. It begins before Nikki Grace can become a stable center: a record needle, the phrase “Axxon N.”, a Polish hotel room and then the Lost Girl crying in front of a television. The opening feels like several wrong beginnings forced into the same body. The machinery is old and weirdly ceremonial. The rabbits appear with a laugh track that makes their room feel less funny than sealed off. The woman in the hotel does not move through the film as much as she is held inside its media, waiting for some opening she cannot name. This is maybe the first place where Lynch’s fragment theory stops sounding spiritual and starts becoming cinematic pressure. The film does not wait for the correct order to arrive. It makes order itself feel like a habit the viewer brought from safer movies. Anderson’s reading helps because she refuses that habit without making the film empty. She writes that she does not want to analyze Inland Empire “as an enigma to be solved or decoded,” which matters because decoding would make the film smaller, almost too consumable. Even if “making sense” of it may be futile, she argues, the film remains legible. The question is where that legibility begins. Room to Dream places the film’s origin in experiment instead of script: Lynch ran into Laura Dern, wrote a scene for her, “didn’t have a feature in mind” and treated it as “just another of his film experiments.” Dern remembers the night as “very magical and a little trancelike,” which could sound like a romantic production anecdote if Inland Empire did not keep showing how frightening a trance can be. The rough digital image makes this frightening from the surface itself. Anderson describes Lynch shooting with a Sony camcorder in “muddy, smudgy digital video,” a low-resolution format that mirrors Nikki’s weakening grip on reality. The cursed remake, the repeated sign of Axxon N. and the opening media trap therefore do not decorate the film with mystery. They make relation happen through blur and broken passage, as if the image can only move toward love or release by first losing the shape that would make it easy to understand.

That passage becomes harder to keep formal once Laura Dern has to carry it. Nikki’s mansion first appears like a space where the film could still behave, polished enough to delay the danger or make it look properly staged, but the Neighbor’s visit immediately ruins that possibility. She enters as if she already belongs to another part of the film, knowing about Nikki’s role before Nikki can comfortably possess it, asking about marriage, telling an “old tale” where a boy passes through a doorway and evil is born. Then the story shifts: a girl is “lost in the marketplace,” “as if half born,” and the alley becomes “the way to the Palace.” The scene gives us prophecy before plot, murder before motivation, time as “today, two days from now, or yesterday.” Its awkward directness matters because Nikki is not receiving information in any ordinary sense. She is being placed inside a passage, or maybe more precisely, made available to one. The role has not swallowed her yet, but the film is already preparing her body for that collapse. Anderson’s argument about Dern keeps this collapse from becoming another closed system signed by one male genius. She writes that Dern “might be thought of as just as much of a creator as Lynch,” and the claim matters because Inland Empire remains legible through performance when its rooms and times stop agreeing with each other. Anderson’s sharper phrase is that “In a project as ungraspable as Inland Empire, Dern’s corporeality functions as the movie’s irreducible reality.” The actress-role collapse, then, becomes a physical method. Nikki becomes Sue, Sue breaks into other damaged voices, and the film keeps returning to Dern’s face because the face can carry what the story refuses to organize. When the Axxon N. sign appears on a decaying door and pulls Nikki/Sue back into the rehearsal space, the passage is spatial, but it also feels bodily. The written mark opens time, and Dern’s movement through it makes repetition look less like structure than possession. Her silent scream pushes that possession into the face itself. Anderson describes Dern’s mouth as becoming “a maw, a portal to an annihilating abyss,” with a scream “no less piercing for its silence.” The face stops behaving like a psychological instrument, one of those faces that politely translates inner life for the viewer. It becomes an opening, and that opening is terrifying because nothing protects it with explanation. This is also where Lynch’s love becomes ethically difficult, maybe even suspicious. Anderson links Nikki to Sandy and Laura Palmer as women sending “an SOS via a look of unremitting pain.” Love, if it appears here at all, begins as attention to that SOS: not saving, not understanding, not romantic recognition, but the brutal act of staying with a damaged image long enough for it to reach someone.

The Hollywood Boulevard death makes that attention even harder to defend because it places the suffering woman directly inside the economy that has been using her. After passing through roles and rooms, Nikki/Sue is stabbed with her own screwdriver and collapses on the Walk of Fame, surrounded by homeless witnesses and the flat cruelty of “You dyin’, lady.” The scene refuses the protection of melodrama. A woman is dying in public, on the surface of Hollywood itself, and then the camera pulls back to reveal the death as part of the film-within-the-film. Anderson’s phrase about “the buying and selling of an actress’s body, her soul” as a commerce that leads to “psychic, if not actual, death” lands here with real force. The actress’s body becomes pain that can still be used as an image. Hollywood can stage death on this body and call it performance. Here Lynch’s spiritual language starts to feel genuinely dangerous, as if any claim about love or unity could become another way of decorating the damage. But Inland Empire does not leave the violence as clean critique either. After the set breaks open, Nikki moves farther inward, through theater and apartment-like spaces, toward the Phantom and Room 47. The film’s architecture starts to feel less like plot than a nervous system, with doors and faces carrying something that has not yet been released. When Nikki confronts and shoots the Phantom, the distorted face that appears back at her does not give the satisfaction of a villain defeated. It feels more like an image forced to show its own corruption. Evil is not solved here. The trapped image has just been pushed far enough to change form. The meeting with the Lost Girl gives that change its most fragile shape. Nikki reaches the room connected to the rabbits and to the crying woman who has been watching from the beginning; the women kiss and embrace, Nikki fades, and the Lost Girl leaves confinement to reunite with her husband and son. The kiss cannot really behave as romantic fulfillment. It works more like recognition, or a transfer between damaged selves that were never fully separate. Anderson’s description of the film as “a hinged altarpiece to imperilled women at once brutal and tender” makes sense here, because tenderness arrives only after the film has made the viewer sit with violence until it stops being consumable. In Room to Dream, Lynch says the human trip has “the happiest of happy endings,” but Inland Empire makes that phrase difficult to accept in any ordinary way. Happiness here is the moment when one trapped consciousness can finally pass through another and leave.

The lithographs shift the argument into a different scale, where the cinematic labyrinth is reduced to paper, stone, barely alive figures. This reduction, however, does not make Lynch any less “difficult”. In the films, the viewer can blame confusion on plot or dream structure; in the lithographs there is very little to hide behind. A blunt phrase appears beside a rough body, blackness presses around it. Rodley’s reminder that painting is “where everything begins for Lynch” is key here, because these prints return to a more primitive problem of visuality as opposed to cinematic stills, how to make a body appear on a surface without pretending that the surface is neutral. Rodley describes Lynch’s later canvases as “dark, scumbled surfaces” where “spindly characters” are stranded in a field of black paint and detritus. The lithographs keep this pressure, but the printed mark makes this confinement quieter. The figure sits there as if the paper has caught it in the middle of some private damage.

I Write on Your Skin How Much I Love You is probably the clearest place to begin because the title already sounds embarrassingly direct, like bad romantic language, and then the word “skin” ruins the softness of it. Love has to be written somewhere. More specifically, it has to be written on a body. The title turns intimacy into inscription and makes affection look dangerously close to injury, which is why the work fits Lynch’s late logic better than any clean image of tenderness could. In Inland Empire, Nikki/Sue’s body carries roles until performance and suffering become hard to separate; here, the body becomes a surface before any story can protect it. Love is pushed outward, made visible as pressure on skin, and that pressure is both message and wound.

A Lonely Figure Talks to Himself Softly takes the problem in another direction. The title gives us speech, but speech that barely travels. Someone talks softly, and the softness already sounds like failure, because no receiver is promised. The loneliness is formal before it turns psychological: a figure isolated on the page, being held by blackness and speaking into a space that may only reciprocate his own voice. Lynch’s comments on black help make sense of this. “Black has depth,” he says, and because it “keeps on continuing to be dark, the mind kicks in.” Then comes the turn that feels central to the prints: “You start seeing what you’re afraid of. You start seeing what you love.” Blackness is not background in this logic, it, on the contrary, activates looking, makes the viewer’s mind enter the image and fill its silence with something private of their own. Lynch also says his painted figures are “like fragments of a body,” and that he “can’t do a whole figure.” The broken figure can receive more projection than a complete one. When Rodley suggests that these bodies look as if they are trying to get out of the picture and Lynch answers, “They’re in trouble, maybe,” the uncertainty keeps the trouble from becoming anecdote, and thus, the figure is trapped materially, inside the flatness of the image itself.

Moving onto the medium, which makes this “entrapment” less metaphorical. Lynch worked at Idem in Paris, with old presses and Solnhofen stone, and the history of the place mattered to him enough that he said, “I fell in love.” That sentence would be too sentimental if the process were not so physical. Lithography depends on pressure, reversal and the stone’s capacity to hold a mark; FRAC describes used stones as supports where erased images can still “haunt” like “ghostly remnants.” The support remembers – quite literally. In that sense, a work like Man Floating in Room Alone makes the room into a printed condition, an interior held in flattened matter, while the floating body suggests release without giving it. FRAC’s claim that lithographic inversion becomes an “inversion of worlds,” and that stone for Lynch is “a threshold, a passage, a vehicle,” brings the prints back to Inland Empire through another kind of connection. The film moves through doors and digital blur; the lithographs move through stone, reversed writing and black fields that keep taking images in. Love in these works becomes smaller than the film’s final kiss, more exposed in its awkwardness: a phrase on skin, a voice with no listener, or a body alone in a room – very similar to Beckett’s late prose. It survives as a mark that keeps trying to reach another surface.

The final kiss in Inland Empire brings the argument back to the very ordinary desire for suffering to organize itself into something readable. For a second, Lynch seems to give release or freedom in a form we can recognize: one woman reaches another, the room opens, the trapped figure goes home. But the image is too damaged by everything that came before to become a simple and comprehensible closure. Nikki/Sue has passed through death, performance and a face that stopped behaving like a face; the Lost Girl has been watching from inside media itself, crying in front of a television as if waiting for an image to become strong enough to touch her. So when love appears, it does not look like romance. It looks like a body becoming a vessel for another body’s freedom.

The lithographs make the same promise smaller and even more awkward. A sentence writes love on skin. A lonely figure talks softly to no one. A man floats in a room alone. None of these images are happy in any clean sense, but they keep insisting that damaged form can still send something outward. Lynch’s esotericism matters here because it gives brokenness a way to stay broken without becoming mute. The viewer is trained to want meaning to arrive like the fake robin in Blue Velvet, visible enough to eat darkness in one obedient gesture. Now, late Lynch gives something less safe. Love survives as inscription, as blur, as a voice that may not reach anyone in time. And maybe that is his most serious version of the “happiest of happy endings”: the world is not repaired into clarity, but the image still tries to pass through.
