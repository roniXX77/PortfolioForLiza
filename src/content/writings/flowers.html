The Greatest USPS Commercial

Liz Novikova’s opinion on the only Jim Jarmusch’s movie she didn’t want to watch

Apart from Bill Murray and Tilda Swinton – who, let’s be honest, some people were already kind of tired of – Broken Flowers barely even feels like a Jim Jarmusch film. Compared to the philosophical-noir depth of Dead Man, the sly banality of Coffee and Cigarettes, or the effortlessly stylish Night on Earth, this one comes off as, at best, bland – or worse, flirting with the kind of easy entertainment you’d expect from Desperate Housewives. And honestly, even Desperate Housewives might’ve made more sense at Cannes in 2005: those classic early-2000s melodramas were at least trying to nod toward some kind of social relevance. Broken Flowers doesn’t even bother. From the opening frames, set to soft soul and scrubbed clean of any texture or risk, the film looks like a slick USPS commercial that blew its entire budget on being one hour and forty minutes long instead of saying anything at all.

And yet, we sit through it. We let ourselves fall for this Jarmuschian counterfeit charm, and we watch it all the way to the end – just like Murray’s character, drifting through his quiet bachelor quest with no real reason to care, no real motivation, following a 20-year-old breakup letter all the way to a dead-end finale. And like him, we are left standing there after the credits, completely lost – but with the faintest smile, like someone who just realized they’ve been kindly tricked by a fairy tale. And maybe that’s okay.

Broken Flowers really is a fairy tale – and not in some vague atmospheric way, but in the most literal, literary sense. The protagonist, Don Johnston (with a “T”), doesn’t even try to hide the stupidity of his parallel with Don Juan. He’s literally watching an old, terribly acted TV adaptation of Don Juan while the other characters praise his legendary romantic past, from the film’s opening moments all the way to the end. These kinds of laurels, in postmodern quasi-realistic cinema, feel totally unearned – more like hyper-romanticized old Hollywood, where the director seems weirdly insistent on convincing us of a cliché rather than building a real character. It’s not personality, it’s archetype.

So, our archetypal fairy-tale hero receives a fairy-tale pink letter from a past lover about a long-lost son and sets off on a “hero’s journey” to find out who sent it. Naturally, he’s joined by his fairy-tale sidekick – his Ethiopian neighbor Winston – who cracks the clues and plans the route. And honestly, it’s hard to tell what’s more of a literary gimmick here: his cartoonish Sherlock routine (Don literally calls him Sherlock multiple times), the fact that he shares a name with both a woman’s dog and a shady cigarette brand, or the absurd coexistence of his exaggerated reggae-ethnic-Ethiopian flavor with the image of a classically white, 19th-century European detective.

But the storybook absurdity doesn’t stop there. Jarmusch throws around names like confetti: from a teenage seductress named Lolita to a woman named after Neil Young’s “Sun Green.” And just when you might want to believe in the narrative, like you would in a genuinely good story, he drags you right back out with meaningless on-camera conversations about names – flattening the characters into perfectly cut pop culture tropes you can’t help but recognize.

But the thing about fairy tales is that they only work when the characters belong to the same universe. And here’s the catch: not only does every trope feel brazenly out of place, but the story itself is missing all the ingredients that could make it even remotely believable. Don has zero motivation or desire to go on this so-called hero’s journey for the entire runtime – aside from the ending, maybe. Or maybe Bill Murray only started wanting to act once the end of filming was in sight. Either way, the character feels more like an actor stuck in a midlife existential crisis – or some superhero animator who’s wandered into the wrong script. He openly admits he doesn’t want to do anything, and couldn’t care less – and we’re already thirty minutes in. Meanwhile, Sherlock (aka Winston) is planning the entire trip, from the plane tickets to the idea itself, feeding Don such painfully overused, mechanical lines that Murray’s character doesn’t come off as indifferently real or natural – just absurd. It’s like his presence in the film isn’t even justified – he’s being pushed forward by some invisible authorial hand, lodged inside Winston’s awkward, ill-fitting lines. Picture this: Don Juan, weary and completely disinterested, passively resisting a storyline about housewives reflecting on their flings from twenty years ago – a storyline with no real stakes, no real pace, and a conclusion no one cares about because no one cares about the protagonist. Got it? Now just accept the fact that thanks to costume designer John Dunn and set decorator Lydia Marks, our Don Juan is actually an aging Bill Murray in a track jacket and sweatpants, sipping Château Moet on a threadbare couch somewhere in the middle of New Jersey. Broken Flowers is – quite literally – a broken fairy tale. It manufactures flatness out of nothing. The plot, the characters, the acting, the supposed intrigue – it all fades into the background, becoming just a set dressing for something that, in a narrative film, usually doesn’t exist on its own.

The first of these elements: the setting and mise-en-scène, which seem to have a life of their own – often far more interesting than Murray’s bored expression. In Winston’s home, your eyes dart between Bob Marley posters, a fridge plastered with magnets, shelves collapsing under magazines and books, and an overall curated chaos. Broken Flowers is filled with a kind of clutter: from Lolita and Laura’s (Sharon Stone) sequin-covered, tchotchke-strewn house, to Penny’s crumbling mud roads and fabric-stuffed trailers, there’s zero cinematic elegance or even any visible logic to how the props are used in each scene. And yet, somehow, that mess creates a strange comfort – a sense that something in the film is actually alive, and working, almost subconsciously. The mise-en-scène starts breathing on its own the moment it becomes the only thing moving the story forward. Instead of the narrative shaping the space, it’s the space that begins to forecast the narrative: Tilda Swinton’s character turns out to be white trash and her scene ends in Don getting punched; Lolita and Laura’s house culminates in seduction; Carmen’s office leads only to awkward silence. The visuals know more than the script does. And somehow, in all that visual excess, there’s something gently surreal – as if Jarmusch is quietly trying to show us how rich and strange the world can be around someone whose ego was designed to be consumed by others.

Second: the music. Jarmusch throws in Ethiopian jazz like someone asked him to host a cultural appreciation night. And knowing his almost sacred relationship with sound – from experimental SQÜRL noise to Ukrainian lutes and Wu-Tang Clan – you want to believe Ethiopian jazz means something here, that there’s narrative weight behind it. But no. For better or worse, this Southern sound doesn’t quite mesh with the cold New Jersey landscapes, Don’s failed romantic-heroic itinerary, or the cheap rental cars he floats through. And the way each track is dropped – as literal mixtape cuts playing from a car stereo – makes the placement feel random, causeless, like we’re being forced to listen along from the passenger seat of someone else’s life. Winston isn’t even a real character here – not someone the plot needs. He’s a dummy doll of a sidekick, created solely so Jarmusch could play his current musical obsession on screen. It’s almost like he wrote Winston just to have an excuse to soundtrack this film with the Ethiopian jazz he was deep into during production. And somehow, this makes the whole thing even more disorienting. But also – strangely moving. Because it’s fanatical. It’s someone’s private obsession bleeding into the film – and it pulls us, for a moment, out of the confusion and into something bigger and greater than cinema itself: jazz.

And just like the music, the women in Broken Flowers seem to exist outside the film’s logic – or rather, in spite of it. They’re not characters, they are surreal American archetypes with professions so absurdly specific they feel pulled from the satire section of an early-2000s lifestyle magazine. Sharon Stone drives a truck and moonlights as a wardrobe consultant for the wealthy. Jessica Lange is an animal communicator, too busy to talk to a human because an iguana is about to arrive. Tilda Swinton lives on a biker-infested dirt lot straight out of a post-apocalyptic Sons of Anarchy spinoff. These professions don’t deepen the characters: they exaggerate their unreality. Each woman becomes less of a person and more of a stylized absurd symbol. Their jobs aren’t reflections of who they are, but rather weird, cheap placeholders in a movie where everything – from the set design to the soundtrack – seems to be moving forward on its own, dragging the main character along like a paper doll.

Finally, the last piece: protest.

Even in the haze of confusion and general lack of motivation, even for viewers who’ve only seen one or two Jarmusch films, Broken Flowers reveals itself as a carefully engineered antonym to his own cinematic language. Each scene, in terms of color and texture, looks more like a TV show than anything from the noir-obsessed, analog-loving Jarmusch we thought we knew. And the pink? That strange, reckless pink? Feels almost provocatively off-brand for the guy who gave us Coffee and Cigarettes. But the pink screams with life, independence, brightness – it doesn’t need the director to pull our attention, just like tea doesn’t need to be coffee, and weed doesn’t need to be a cigarette. All these aesthetic choices flirt with bad taste – but so boldly, so visually loud, that it becomes clear: the disorientation of space, the lack of narrative logic, the fairy tale that refuses to be one – it’s all Jarmusch staging a protest. Not just against himself, but against his own obsessive cinematic polish and signature control. The clues to the letter’s mystery are everywhere – color-coded, spoken aloud, teased and dangled in front of us – but the more the protagonist tries to piece them together into some coherent narrative, the more they slip through his fingers. In the end, they lead him not to the truth, but to a random boy on the street who turns out not to be his son. This nonsense – all dressed up in a deliberately wrong visual code – isn’t just rebellion against Jarmusch. It's a rebellion against cinema itself, in the name of boredom, banality, and the flatness of life. And that flatness? That’s protest too – against Cannes, which, like Don Johnston, stuck inside their own epic storylines, can’t cope with real life when it actually arrives.

And so, having lost track of the maybe-son, Don stares into the face of some random guy driving by in a car. The film ends. It makes no sense. The audience at Cannes shifts uncomfortably in their seats, wondering what that was all about, trying to make peace with the broken fairy tale they just sat through for two hours. And most won’t notice – buried deep in the credits – that the guy in the car was played by none other than Homer Murray, the real-life son of the actor playing Don.

"Broken Flowers needs no narrative," writes Richard A. Blake – and he’s right. However loud and exhausting Don’s apathy and his mind-numbingly stupid road trip may seem, his brokenness somehow generates a quiet melancholy, even a desire to reflect – more than it provokes irritation. Peter Bradshaw called Broken Flowers “a bold venture into the mainstream… that creates a gentle cloud of happiness,” while A.O. Scott in The New York Times didn’t sugarcoat it: “A Bittersweet Trip to the Land of Lost Love.”

And that’s exactly what it is: a dysfunctional, messy, illusory image that, by the end, gives the attentive viewer a breath of fresh air – and a quiet piece of advice from someone who accidentally tested the fairy tale and lived to tell about it.

And finally, the question that comes up every time Johnston carefully assembles yet another bouquet before his next encounter: Why is the film called Broken Flowers? Originally, Jarmusch’s script was titled Dead Flowers, but the director kept searching for something more precise, more resonant. And he found it. Broken Flowers is an absurd, oxymoronic image – and yet, for some reason, it feels emotionally clear and strangely intimate within the light, drifting tone of this film. It stands for missed chances, lost moments, the beautiful things in a life that can’t be recovered – but it doesn’t close the door. It gently leaves it ajar.

And in doing so, Jarmusch breaks cinema as a medium just enough – not to destroy it, but to reshape it. What we get in the end is not quite a film, but a quiet, melancholic sketch where color, set design, music, and marginalia become the real protagonist.

And when you think about it, this is exactly that same advertising logic we’ve all gotten used to: the illusion of a fairy tale, shown so obviously to be fake that it makes you want to buy the product out of sheer despair. And the only – but most important – thing that sets Jim Jarmusch’s film apart from everything it quietly mimics is the broken pink flowers. A soft reminder that the fake fairy tale always feels sweeter – because underneath it lives something painfully ordinary, stirred by a cool New Jersey breeze: real life.
