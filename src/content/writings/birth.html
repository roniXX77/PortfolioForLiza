Theoretical musing on the subject of frustration

Critics are frustrated taxidermists.

James Broughton[1]

Really, my experience with artists has been that, generally speaking, one-half step outside their own field and they are much more conservative and malicious than George Bush.

Stan Brakhage[2]

Flipping through Broughton’s manifesto of cinema as a way of seeing, I am asking myself: had he thought at all about his old buddy/ex-housemate Stan Brakhage as a source of inspiration – or, could it be that Brakhage stole his sensitivity to happening and momentum from Broughton, transforming it into, in my perspective, a much more valuable idea of happening and making as inherently interconnected cinematic experiences? Either of that, or maybe Broughton’s idea of “life as long undressing” is not far from the way Brakhage looks/sees his wife giving birth in Window Water Baby Moving (1959). The question I am going back again and again to is, does Brakhage look or see, as per James Broughton? Does he, as many of the “frustrated taxidermists” say, intend to neglect his wife in a voyeurist way, fetishize, disembody through camera? Is there a kind of sacredness he disturbed by deciding to film a child being born instead of straightforward pornography? And, an even bigger question – is there a way in which birth and filmmaking engage in semiotically, both as different manifestations of human creation, that somehow must offend the spectator, no matter political views?

There is this temptation of the flat interpretation of Stan Brakhage as a masculine spectator translating his male gaze through the means of camera, making us look at childbirth on a big screen in 16 mm. It is so simple to jump to conclusions about sexist imagery once we see female nudity, let alone a specific woman in almost a documentary mode of photography. Yet, I do recognize that the tenderness of Brakhage’s gaze does not necessarily erase the asymmetry between Jane’s experience of childbirth and his authority over its cinematic representation. I think that’s because cinema is by default a self-reflective medium, like a mirror, and the spectator’s mind trained to be passive, sleeping on their own centuries-long fetishes, can’t seem to recognize their own biases and sexualization because they are so used to fast and bulletproof crowd pleasure.[3] The truth is, a spectator gets terribly offended when their spectatorship is exposed and turned against them without an extra layer of fetishization. Not just that, but we seem to forget how normal and physiological an experience of childbirth is. A spectator looks at Window Water Baby Moving with disgust, because they’re used to watch and identify solely pornographic visuals, and with terror, because they are so disconnected from their own real-life experience that they are simply scared of any bodily experience happening on the screen. For what it’s worth, I found the film incredibly beautiful from the first seconds until the end, and not because I am trying to assert my superiority in understanding experimental film, but because I rarely see an “experimental” film that doesn’t seem grossed out by female body and character.

In his “Metaphors on vision”, Brakhage treats infancy with tenderness you rarely see, understanding childhood as a special moment of time, a moment of first recognition or first knowledge.[4] One has to see that the nudity he exposes connotes a different type of vulnerability. It is simply non-sexual, because it is protected by the images of the window, the sunshine, the momentum of birth – the visuality of new beginning, filled with oxygen, as tormentful as it feels for Jane to take a breath – it is this type of vulnerability that is beautiful in its unawareness, because there is an opening to learn, to come to existence, to see, in many ways, like camera rolling or film developing. The film that Brakhage is making essentially shows us life happening in front of us, disrupting the border of the theater screen awareness, and – taking us back to the realization of being present not in this room, but in this life – what could be more rude than that, right?

And then of course, there is divine nature to imagery of motherhood. Motherhood is sacred, sealed for the religious books, and is definitely not supposed to be shown to masses, because – well, why exactly? Mary is an important biblical figure more because she bears Christ as her child than because she is a mother or a woman. Brakhage, on the other hand, seems to like both – he shows some sexuality of Jane as her own pleasure and bodily agency that hides between the sunrays but is not anyhow juxtaposed to the process of childbirth; nor is she rejected as a mother, and, to be honest, we barely see the child itself – Jane Brakhage in this movie is saint because she is a person capable of experiencing the cycle of life (and death) and its physicality, not because of underlying divination serving the conservative narrative of motherhood.

After all, as gruesome as it may sound, the avant-garde's supposed openness to sexual experimentation did not necessarily translate into an equally open attitude toward women's bodies and experiences. Even within experimental cinema, the question of whose sexuality could be celebrated and whose artistic contributions could be recognized remained deeply gendered (remember Menken's relative invisibility compared to Maas. And I have to say that I understand Broughton’s notion of seeing from the beginning of this note as being able to pass through sociocultural obstacles of finding beauty of life experience, and I think there is no better movie to exemplify seeing than Window Water Baby Moving.
