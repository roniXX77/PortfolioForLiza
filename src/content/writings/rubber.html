Homage To No Reason

My friends and I have a tradition: I, a snob known in certain circles, invite people over at the most inconvenient hour (never before midnight), cast a proud gaze, and choose the next movie by yet another of my “favorite directors” – the very ones who once drove Cannes audiences out of the theater in offended protest. For the next two hours, I scold anyone who dares interrupt the screening. Afterward, we usually linger until four, even six in the morning, luxuriating in our discussion of this “intellectual” movie that everyone claims to understand – so much so that no one dares make the first move home, despite early classes the next day. But once the screening ends – and I approach its analysis (not without a tremor of fear) – all my guests scatter home before the curfew. Not because the runtime is a mere hour and a half, as one might assume; not because the horror genre (as Wikipedia cheerfully notes) is excessively brutal; nor even because the ending is utterly incoherent, with a banal Hollywood landmark serving as mere backdrop for the protagonist. It’s far simpler: whatever we try to discuss or dissect was shown to us on screen, word for word, mere moments before.

My friends are ordinary viewers; they love watching thrillers in good company and couldn’t care less about Cannes’ “official selections.” Indie cinema, accustomed to shocking such audiences with extravagance, has grown somewhat tiresome. Yet Quentin Dupieux’s movie Rubber seems to exhaust even a devoted fan’s vocabulary. It’s terrifying to speak of it – once you pin a mask of absurdity and unseriousness onto a movie, it starts speaking to the viewer with alarming volume and venom. One feels ashamed to write about such a movie, but we’ll try – perhaps we’ll prove especially perceptive.

First: the unseriousness. Rubber can be summarized thus: this thriller with a double-entendre title tells the story of a living, murderous tire that obliterates people’s heads with telepathic force and stalks a young woman across the desert out of… love, while the police attempt to apprehend it. And former Parisian DJ Quentin Dupieux – who serves as director, cinematographer, writer, and editor – makes a movie about spectators who, in that same desert, watch the thriller through binoculars. It seems the director is either brazenly clowning about, throwing his budget to the winds and forgetting the audience, or openly mocking those who spent their weekend on a movie outing. If you are left with zero desire to press “Play” after a description like that, believe me – you will be understood.

What unfolds onscreen is theater of the absurd, or a hallucination – not the horror genre advertised. Instead of tense music, Dupieux scores the movie with his old DJ tracks; a bizarre man on a bicycle feeds hungry viewers American turkey in the desert; and for the first fifteen of eighty minutes, they sit entranced, watching a tire crawl across the landscape in documentary style à la Discovery Channel – dragging us into their gaze. Dupieux strips us of all dignity: first, by dangling a hollow promise and reducing us from refined cinephiles to gullible gawkers; then, by inserting his own on-screen audience, brazenly usurping our role. We are watching a movie, yet we are no longer the viewers – we are spectators watching other spectators, convinced of their own reality, blind to their part in this performance. Do we ourselves understand?

If you don’t shut off the movie after that delightful slap in the face, other questions arise. In Rubber, there is no place – only its abstraction: a generic cactus-strewn desert, a motel of no particular character, a gas station, a couple of old cars. These could be the backlot of any B-grade Hollywood movie, a location the viewer’s mind wants to trust for its comforting American dreaminess. The temptation is overwhelming – until it’s exposed and rubbed in with equal speed, as the viewer sees close-ups of scattered trash and spectators dying from poisoned Thanksgiving turkey. This isn’t a cinematic space; it is its absence, a void where audience and actors coexist and could, if they wished, simply stroll onto the set.

Characters and plot scarcely bother to introduce themselves. The movie features: viewers whose motives are unclear and who, logically, shouldn’t be there; a stereotypical cop reading his lines from a sheet; a manic pixie girl on a road trip, devoid of backstory or dialogue despite being the object of affection; a cowboy-hatted motel owner, a chambermaid – and the tire, which one hesitates to call a “character.” There is also the bicyclist, the organizer of this desert screening – he tries to recount his childhood at minute fifty, only to die seconds later. No one pays attention; his story plays no part in the movie (at least one of its halves). Among these non-characters unfolds a non-plot: they watch the movie, and nothing else happens. Naturally, one actor “breaks” before the audience, which leads to the non-plot’s further non-development, since its non-reality becomes evident. The movie’s nothingness grows painfully awkward, for, in irony, we are openly told about real actors and a real classical thriller plot – visible right before our eyes. Someone might snort, “This isn’t cinema!” But what else is there for non-viewers to watch?

Quentin Dupieux makes no secret of his design. His movie is an “homage to ‘no reason’” (yes, quoted verbatim in the opening five minutes). Rubber feels like a sharp experiment on the viewer. Throughout, you will ask yourself: “Why these chairs at the start?”; “What is this tire and how does it work?”; “Why use a plastic doll to kill the tire?”; “Why so slow?” – all the useless, stupid questions, except those that concern cinema as art. I tested this on five friends – it never fails. But don’t worry, they won’t pester you for long: the on-screen spectators will voice their questions for you in real time and even conduct a philosophical dialogue on talking and piracy inside a movie theater, turning the whole scenario into a comic absurdity that leaves you even more disoriented.

Then, in the final five minutes, just when you are ready to give up and tear your hair out from this infernal experience, the tire finally reaches the first non-abstract object in the movie: a Hollywood sign – set to trendy music – and resolutely begins its conquest. Credits roll; a collective sigh of relief. We have understood again. We have just watched a brilliant indie movie about cinema’s metaphysical game!

Movie critics both fear and adore Dupieux for his bluntness wrapped in surreal comedy. Peter Bradshaw wrote that “The sheer, insouciant silliness of this film is engaging”, while Manohla Dargis managed to throw around a few bolder statements, calling the tire “a totem of American industrial, carelessly forgotten” and even getting fascinated by how “you can count the treads and even see it breathe”. “Unsettled times demand absurdity – a genre in which Quentin is today the unquestioned star”, – astutely notes Inna Denisova. As much as I appreciate good writing on cinema (and love to debate with critics), today I will offer only one bow in their direction: dear critics, armchair or New Yorker alike, Rubber is dedicated to you as well! So, however thrilled you feel at penning fresh criticism, please remember – you’ve been made fools before the credits even roll – but done so lovingly and with such charm that you lack the nerve to tear Dupieux’s little prank to shreds.

I won’t lie – it is a pleasure to arrive at the conclusion of this absurdist satire shot just to spite Hollywood’s masters and critique all narrative cinema. There really is no desire to hold a discussion afterward. Yet one scene lingers days, weeks later: when the cop, believing the audience poisoned and dead, tries to convince his fellow officers to call it off. “It’s over – relax, it’s not real,” he cries in desperation, then shoots himself to prove that everything unfolding is mere cinema. The other police officers struggle to believe him, and the tire’s victim’s corpse remains chillingly inert – because one spectator survived, and the movie will go on as long as there’s at least one viewer. I can’t forget the cop’s sigh of relief when his timer runs out, nor the hollow pain in his eyes when he realizes the movie isn’t over. In his movie The Second Act, fourteen years later, Dupieux says that life is cinema turned inside out. The tragedy of not being able to say “it’s not real” – that is life. A vast, terrifying tragedy.

For no reason.
