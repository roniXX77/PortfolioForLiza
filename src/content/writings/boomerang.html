Elizaveta Novikova

One Second Too Late: Phenomenology On the Verge of Text and Video in 1970s

What interests me about Boomerang is that it seems to produce a contradiction at the level of the medium itself. Video is often associated with immediacy, transmission, with the possibility of collapsing distance between an event and its reception. Yet Richard Serra and Nancy Holt’s Boomerang (1974) turns that promise into a problem: the work is built around a delay of roughly one second: Holt wears headphones and speaks while hearing her own voice fed back to her slightly later. The gap is almost insignificant in measurable terms, and still it reorganizes the entire experience of speaking. Watching the video, I found myself less captivated by what Holt says than by what happens to speech once it can no longer remain synchronized with itself and becomes a separated entity. The work seems to ask a simple question: what happens when the present shifts in time?

Visually, very little appears to happen. Holt sits in a recording studio, framed closely and wearing a technician's headset. There is no dramatic action, narrative progression, nor theatrical staging that would normally organize the viewer's gaze. In some ways, the image almost resists longitudinal attention. Yet the longer one watches, the more this visual restraint begins to feel necessary. The work's event is not hidden behind the image, nor is it represented symbolically through it. Rather, the image functions as a kind of support structure for a process unfolding elsewhere, in the unstable relation between voice and transmission. The headphones become the most captivating object in the frame exactly for the reasons that they make visible an otherwise invisible circuit; i.e., they point toward a process rather than an image.

This also helps further explain why Howard Wise's discussion of early video feels relevant here. Writing about the emergence of Electronic Arts Intermix, Wise contextualizes video within a broader shift from the Machine Age to what he calls the Electronic Era, a moment when artists began treating television technology as an artistic medium rather than merely a vehicle for commercial broadcasting. What matters here along with the historical context is the question of medium-specificity that emerges from it. Television had long borrowed structures from theatre, literature, painting and film; Boomerang, by contrast, seems determined to strip away those inherited frameworks. Performance remains, but does so only in a reduced form. Holt is not acting a role nor developing a character. Instead, the apparatus itself generates the structure of the work, in a way the delay mechanism cannot be understood as a neutral recording device, as it produces the conditions under which the performance becomes possible.

The more I think about it, the more alienated and grotesque that one-second delay becomes. Ordinarily, hearing oneself speak feels so immediate that it disappears from conscious experience. Speech and self-perception seem to occupy the same temporal space. Boomerang separates them. Holt hears herself speaking and hears herself having spoken almost simultaneously, which creates a situation where language begins to interfere with itself. The delay is small enough that the viewer can imagine it physically, yet large enough to disrupt continuity. Wise's claim that video possesses its own temporality hence becomes concrete. Video time becomes a sensory experience of temporal dislocation, a condition in which the body can no longer fully inhabit the present moment it is producing.

Rosalind Krauss's reading of Boomerang pushes this problem further because she places it within a larger discussion of feedback and early video's formal logic. For Krauss, video frequently stages a body caught within circuits of transmission and return. Although many of her examples focus on visual feedback, Boomerang transfers that structure into sound. As a result, Holt's body becomes less a performer than a site where technological processes become perceptible. We are not witnessing a dramatic breakdown. In fact, the work's restraint is part of what makes it compelling. The frame remains stable and the setting remains ordinary, and yet speech gradually begins to deconstruct and fracture under the pressure of its own repetition.

Krauss describes the piece as an "extraordinary image of distraction," which struck me as an unusual phrase. Distraction is generally understood as a mental condition, something difficult to visualize directly. Boomerang, on the other hand, manages to construct distraction as a visual and auditory experience. Holt explains that hearing the beginning of a word can cause her to lose the end of it; language becomes interrupted by its own return. Instead of a symbolic failure of communication there is a highly specific and formalist disturbance in the process that emerges. Speech is revealed as something more fragile than it usually appears, where sentence depends upon movement forward through time – and, finally, that movement repeatedly collapses back upon itself.

This is where Krauss's notion of the "prison of a collapsed present" is useful as well. Holt is not separated from some distant historical past; she is separated from the immediate past of her own speech. The words she has just spoken return quickly enough to remain recognizable and yet too late to remain usable: they become detached from intention. In a sense, language acquires a physicality that it does not usually possess. Words stop functioning transparently and begin behaving like objects, things that return from outside rather than expressions emerging from within. The title Boomerang feels excessively literal, nearly ironic. Speech is thrown outward and comes back altered by the process of transmission.

Thinking about the work in relation to performance raises another question. Conventional theatrical forms generally rely upon some prior structure, whether a script, choreography, score or sequence of remembered actions. Such frameworks allow gestures to accumulate meaning over time. Boomerang seems to interfere with that possibility. Holt's speech continually loses access to its own immediate history. The work still contains duration, voice, bodily presence, but performativity becomes subordinated to feedback, resulting in a body negotiating the medium's limits in real time. The artistic method is remarkably simple, yet it results in a surprisingly dense experience of instability.

I was surprised to find that, over time, this made the work feel close to Samuel Beckett's late prose, particularly Company, in my perspective. Beckett often reduces narrative to a voice addressing a body in darkness, creating a situation where memory, perception and selfhood become uncertain, and yet the similarity only goes so far. Beckett stages these tensions through language itself; Boomerang grounds them in a technological process. Holt's condition is not merely existential or linguistic. It is constructed through an apparatus whose operation remains partially visible. The headset, the recording environment and the delayed transmission all ground the experience in the material conditions of the medium. This way, the work transforms a philosophical question into a sensory one.

According to Roshaya Rodness's discussion of delayed auditory feedback, the issue is not that Holt develops a clinical speech disorder. Rather, the work artificially produces a dysfluent condition that reveals how contingent fluent speech already is. We tend to imagine language as evidence of coherent self-presence. Boomerang, on the other hand, complicates that assumption, as Holt's voice remains her own, yet once it returns through the technological circuit it begins to function almost as an external force or field. The boundary between self and other becomes unstable, thus, because transmission reorganizes the experience of inhabiting it.

By the end of the video, I found myself thinking less about speech than about the medium's ability to construct forms of experience. The visual simplicity that initially seemed limiting begins to feel necessary. Nothing distracts from the central process. Holt's body remains in front of us long enough for sound, delay and feedback to acquire a kind of visibility. This is where the work's historical significance seems to be located: rather than using video to document an event, Boomerang turns video's own formal logic into the event itself. It reveals that immediacy is never quite immediate, that transmission always contains the possibility of interruption and that the present, even when technologically produced, can arrive “one second too late”.
